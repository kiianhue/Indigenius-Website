Section two

Stories & Voices

Five conversations with Native healthcare leaders. Each one goes back to where they came from and traces the path that brought them here: what pulled them toward health care, what stood in the way, how they kept going, and what they understand now that they did not understand then. They close by speaking directly to the next generation of Native healthcare leaders.

Episode 01

Dr. Lukejohn Day

Oglala Lakota Nation · Gastroenterologist · Chief Medical Officer, UCSF Health · Professor of Medicine, UCSF

Dr. Day is a physician, researcher, and health-care leader whose work sits at the intersection of medicine, health systems, and Native health. He previously served five years as Chief Medical Officer of Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital and Trauma Center.

He earned his bachelor's degree in chemistry from Yale and his medical degree from Stanford, completed his internal medicine residency and gastroenterology fellowship at UCSF, and in 2024 earned an MBA from the Wharton School. His research focuses on the quality, efficiency, and accessibility of health-care delivery, and on disparities affecting American Indian and Alaska Native communities — including colorectal cancer screening and outcomes. He has served on the governing board of the Association of American Indian Physicians for more than a decade, including as its President.

Questions in this conversation
  • What is a GI doctor, and what drew you to this field?
  • As a gastroenterologist, professor, and chief medical officer — what does a typical week actually look like?
  • What can students do now to reach a career like yours?
  • How has your upbringing as a member of the Oglala Lakota Nation shaped how you navigate healthcare?
  • What does "cultural competence" in healthcare mean to you?
  • What advice do you wish someone had given you before your journey through medicine began?
  • You recently earned an MBA. How has that changed your approach to health care administration?
  • What do you hope for the future of Indigeneity in medicine?

Episode 02

Dr. Yvette Roubideaux

Rosebud Sioux Tribe · Internal Medicine · Former Director, Indian Health Service · Director, Policy Research Center at the National Congress of American Indians · Professor, Colorado School of Public Health

Dr. Roubideaux's work spans clinical medicine, research, teaching, and public health policy. In 2009 she became Director of the Indian Health Service in the Obama Administration, the first woman to lead the agency, overseeing health care for 2.2 million American Indian and Alaska Native people. She later served as Director of the Policy Research Center at the National Congress of American Indians.

She earned her bachelor's degree, her medical degree, and her Master of Public Health all at Harvard, completing her MD in 1989 and her MPH in 1997, with a residency in primary care internal medicine at Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston and the Commonwealth Fund/Harvard University Fellowship in Minority Health Policy.

Her career began in Indian Country rather than in Washington. She spent three years as clinical director and medical officer at the San Carlos Service Unit on the San Carlos Apache Reservation, and a year as a medical officer at Hu Hu Kam Memorial Hospital on the Gila River Indian Reservation, both in Arizona. She went on to teach as assistant professor of family and community medicine at the University of Arizona's College of Medicine and College of Public Health, where she directed two programs recruiting Native students into health and research careers. Her research centers on diabetes prevention in AI/AN communities and on American Indian health policy, and she is a past president of the Association of American Indian Physicians and co-editor of Promises to Keep: Public Health Policy for American Indians and Alaska Natives.

Her conversation is the clearest window on this site into health policy — what it is, how decisions get made, and why Native people need to be in the rooms where they are made.

Questions in this conversation
  • What made you pivot from clinical practice into research, teaching, and policy?
  • Can you walk us through a typical week wearing so many hats — and how do you stay grounded?
  • What are the biggest barriers American Indians face in healthcare, and where should we focus first to improve health equity?
  • If you could change one thing about American Indian health policy in the next five years, what would it be?
  • Beyond becoming a doctor, what paths into healthcare do students overlook?
  • What advice about entering a healthcare journey do you want to share with viewers?
  • What do you hope to see for the future of Native healthcare, for both patients and providers?

Episode 03

Dr. Thomas "Blair" Matheson

Cherokee · Surgeon · Medical Director, National Medical Resources (North Dakota) · President, Association of American Indian Physicians

Dr. Matheson is a physician whose career has been rooted in serving Native American and rural communities. As President of the Association of American Indian Physicians he works to improve Native health and support the next generation of Native physicians. He also serves as the AAIP representative on the American Medical Association's Advisory Committee on American Indian and Alaska Native Affairs.

He is a medical director with National Medical Resources and practices in North Dakota, where his work includes surgery and emergency medicine with a focus on trauma. During the COVID-19 pandemic he volunteered frontline care in Native communities across the West. A Stanford graduate, he is also a professional rodeo cowboy who has competed in team roping and senior breakaway and served as a physician for the National American Indian Rodeo Association.

Questions in this conversation
  • What first made you want to become a physician — was medicine always the plan?
  • How do you carry your identity and community into your work as a health care leader?
  • What does your role as president of the Association of American Indian Physicians involve?
  • What are the biggest barriers Indigenous students face in becoming physicians, and what is AAIP doing about them?
  • Your father helped establish the AAIP and you now serve as its President. Why does it matter so much to have Native physicians shaping Native health care?
  • What advice do you have for Indigenous students who have no doctors in their family or community and feel lost?
  • What do you hope for the future of Indigeneity in medicine?

Episode 04

Dr. Nicole Stern

Mescalero Apache Tribe · Primary Care Physician · Urgent Care Clinical Department Head, Sutter Health Pesetas Urgent Care · Associate Professor of Medical Education, California University of Science and Medicine

Dr. Stern is a physician, educator, researcher, and longtime advocate for the health of American Indian and Alaska Native communities. She earned her medical degree and completed her internal medicine residency at the University of Arizona College of Medicine, completed a fellowship in Primary Care Sports Medicine at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center, and earned a Master of Public Health in Health Management from Harvard.

She has cared for patients in primary and urgent care, worked with athletes at the U.S. Olympic Training Center, and volunteered with the Native Vision program through the Johns Hopkins Center for American Indian Health. Her research has examined health disparities and the underrepresentation of AI/AN people in medicine. She has served as President and on the Board of Directors of the Association of American Indian Physicians, and today is Urgent Care Clinical Department Head at Sutter Health's Pesetas Urgent Care. She is a Fellow of the American College of Physicians and the American Medical Society for Sports Medicine.

Questions in this conversation
  • You are the first physician from your tribe. What did that mean to you personally, and how did you navigate pursuing something no one in your community had done?
  • Why is representation of American Indian and Alaska Native people in medicine so important?
  • After all these years, what qualities make someone a great physician?
  • For viewers who do not know what public health is — what does it involve, and what made you want an MPH?
  • What does "cultural competence" in healthcare mean to you, based on your own experience?
  • What is one piece of advice you hope every young person listening takes away?

Episode 05

Oren Morgan

Otomí · Army veteran · Medical student, Stanford University School of Medicine

Oren Morgan is a Latino and Indigenous medical student, Army veteran, and aspiring surgeon from Northern California. On his mother's side his family is Otomí, an Indigenous people of Guanajuato, Mexico. He was raised by a single mother in a family where resilience was the expectation, and carried forward by mentors who invested in him early. Few people from his background were in medicine, and closing that gap is now part of his work.

He served nine years in the U.S. Army, beginning as an infantryman in the 75th Ranger Regiment and later serving in the California Army National Guard as a sniper team leader, squad leader, and platoon sergeant. He started at a community college in Sacramento, transferred to UCLA, and earned a degree in neuroscience studying sleep, memory, and PTSD. He is now a second-year medical student at Stanford pursuing orthopaedic surgery, general surgery, and functional neurosurgery, with research on the biomechanics and surgical treatment of complex knee injuries in children. At Stanford he is president of the Stanford Medical Student Association for Military Affairs, co-chair of SAIMS (the Indigenous medical student group), and mentorship chair for the Latino Medical Student Association.

Questions in this conversation
  • Walk us through your path from before college to where you are now in medical school.
  • Growing up, did you always see yourself heading into healthcare?
  • How has this unusual path and your identity shaped how you navigate healthcare and your goals?
  • What is the hardest part of medical school that people do not expect?
  • How did you prepare academically and personally for the application process — the MCAT, the personal statement?
  • What would you say to a student who feels they are not "smart enough" or lacks the right background to become a doctor?
  • Is there anything you wish someone had told you before you started down this path?
  • What is a moment in your training that reminded you why you are doing this?
